Overview
The Dungeon World Digital Control Panel is an interactive single-page web application that serves as a comprehensive digital character sheet and control center for the Dungeon World role-playing game. It centralizes all character information, moves, and game management tools in one unified interface, eliminating the need for players to flip through physical sheets or multiple apps during play. This product is designed for Dungeon World players – including solo adventurers and those playing in groups – who want a faster, streamlined way to manage their character and gameplay data.
The control panel provides quick, at-a-glance access to every aspect of the character with minimal navigation, aligning with Dungeon World’s fast-paced, fiction-first gameplay. By automating routine bookkeeping (tracking HP, XP, inventory, etc.) and enforcing the game’s rules, the interface effectively acts as a virtual assistant for the player, handling mechanical details so the player can focus on the narrative and decisions in the game. In short, this product makes playing Dungeon World more efficient and immersive by having all crucial information and actions immediately accessible in one place.
Core Features
•	Character Stats & Health Panel: This fixed heads-up display (typically a left sidebar) shows all essential character stats at all times. It displays the character’s name, class, level, alignment, the six core attributes with their current scores and auto-calculated modifiers, current/maximum HP, current XP (and progress to next level), Armor value, and carry Load. The panel updates dynamically as the game progresses – for example, equipping or removing a piece of gear will automatically update related stats (equipping armor raises the Armor stat, adding a heavy item increases current Load and can flag encumbrance). Debility checkboxes next to each attribute let the player mark temporary penalties (e.g. marking “Weak” applies –1 to STR’s modifier), and the system adjusts the stats immediately to account for the debility. The HP display is prominent, with +/- controls for damage or healing; if HP ever drops to 0, the system instantly prompts the Last Breath move, ensuring that critical rule is not overlooked. This always-on panel is crucial because it gives the player a constant, real-time overview of their character’s status without any extra clicks, so vital information is never missed.
•	Moves Panel and Dice Roller: The Moves panel lists all of the character’s moves – including basic moves, class moves, and any special moves or spells – along with their descriptions. It serves as both a reference and an action interface. For any move that requires a roll, the panel provides one-click execution: the player can click the move, and the system will automatically roll the appropriate dice (typically 2d6 plus the relevant stat modifier) and display the result. The outcome is clearly shown (with success, partial success, or failure indicated), and on a failure roll (6 or below) the system automatically adds +1 XP to the character’s XP tally per Dungeon World rules. This panel streamlines the core gameplay actions – there’s no need to manually grab dice or calculate modifiers, as the app handles it instantly. Move details can be viewed in full (e.g. via a quick pop-up) so the player can read the exact text of a move when needed. By integrating a dice roller directly with the moves, the control panel ensures actions are resolved swiftly and correctly, keeping gameplay momentum high.
•	Inventory Panel (Carried Items): The inventory panel displays all items the character is carrying but not currently equipped. Each item entry shows its name, quantity, and weight (the app automatically calculates total weight for stacked items). The player can add new items via a form (entering details like name, description, weight, tags, number of uses, etc.), edit existing item entries, or remove items as needed. This panel is directly tied to the character’s Load stat – it continuously sums the total carried weight and compares it against the character’s carrying capacity, instantly indicating if the character becomes encumbered or overburdened. Consumable items with limited uses are tracked here as well (for example, a healing potion with 3 uses will decrement each time it’s used). Equipping an item from the inventory (marking it as “equipped”) automatically moves it out of the inventory list and into the Equipment panel. The Inventory panel ensures resource tracking is accurate and effortless, eliminating manual calculations and preventing oversights in carry weight or item usage.
•	Equipment Panel (Equipped Gear): The equipment panel lists all items that the character currently has equipped or in use (such as weapons, armor, or other gear being worn). Items appear here when the player marks them as equipped (e.g. toggling a checkbox on an item in the Inventory panel). The Equipment panel shows each item’s key details and ensures that any effects of equipped gear are applied to the character – for instance, equipping a piece of armor automatically adds its value to the character’s Armor stat, and equipping a weapon might note its damage bonus. Unequipping an item (unchecking it) removes it from the Equipment list and returns it to Inventory, with all stats updating accordingly. This separation of carried vs. equipped items keeps the interface focused: the player can instantly see what gear is actively contributing to their stats. Managing equipment is seamless and quick, so swapping a weapon or armor mid-game is as simple as one click. The system immediately reflects such changes in the relevant stats, ensuring the character’s attributes are always up-to-date.
•	Session Tools (Dice Roller, Trackers, Notes): The session tools are accessible via a toggleable sidebar (drawer) on the interface, providing auxiliary utilities for play. When opened, this panel offers a dice roller for any custom rolls outside of moves (for example, rolling damage dice or other random events). The user can specify the type of dice and quantity (d4, d8, d10, etc.) and see the result instantly, with results logged alongside move rolls. The panel also includes trackers that the player can create for various purposes – e.g. to track ongoing spell effects, countdown clocks, or “hold” points from certain moves. Each tracker is a small counter with a title that the user can increment or decrement; the interface may highlight a tracker when it hits zero or mark it as complete. Additionally, a scratchpad-style notes area is available for jotting down short-term notes or reminders during the session (such as NPC names, clues, or mission objectives). The Session Tools panel stays hidden when not needed and can be opened with one click whenever these utilities are required. This design keeps the main screen uncluttered while still giving the player quick access to dice and note-keeping functions, essentially replicating the convenience of having extra paper, tokens, or dice at the table in a digital form.
•	Journal Panel (Session Logs): The journal panel provides an organized space for long-form notes and session logging. Here, the player can create timestamped journal entries to record what happened during each game session or to note important story developments. The UI allows writing and editing rich-text entries (for example, an entry titled “Session 5 – The Dragon’s Lair” with a narrative summary of that session’s events). Entries are stored chronologically, serving as a campaign diary that the player can review at any time to recall past events. After each session, the application will prompt the player to add a journal entry, encouraging consistent record-keeping of the story. This panel is separate from the quick scratchpad notes – it’s meant for more permanent, structured documentation. The Journal enhances long-term engagement by preserving the narrative history of play, which is valuable for continuity and immersion across multiple sessions.
•	Integrated Rule Automation: The control panel actively enforces Dungeon World’s rules and automations to assist the player. Many tedious calculations are handled automatically: the app computes ability modifiers from ability scores, applies debility penalties whenever a debility checkbox is toggled, keeps track of current Load vs. max Load to warn of encumbrance, and checks XP against the level-up threshold so it knows when the character qualifies to level up. Whenever the player triggers a move roll, the system not only rolls the dice but also handles outcomes like marking XP on a miss without any extra input. At the end of each session, the app guides the user through the End of Session move – it asks the required questions about resolved bonds, alignment fulfillment, and the three end-of-session questions, automatically awarding the correct XP for each “Yes” answer. If the accumulated XP meets or exceeds the required amount to level up (current level + 7 XP), the system immediately notifies the player and walks them through a Level Up process (increasing the character’s level, resetting or reducing XP, choosing a new move, and improving an ability score). All these automated features ensure important steps are never forgotten and that game mechanics (XP gain, stat changes, item effects, etc.) are consistently applied. The player remains in control – the interface will confirm major changes like leveling up – but the heavy lifting of rule-tracking is handled by the software, keeping gameplay smooth and error-free.
User Experience
Our primary user persona is a Dungeon World player using this application as their personal digital character sheet and adventure toolkit. The interface is designed to support the user through every stage of play, from preparing for a session, through the heat of gameplay, to the end-of-session wrap-up. The goal is a seamless user journey where the player always knows where to find information or perform an action, with the UI guiding them naturally. Below is the typical flow of a game session and how the control panel enhances each phase of the experience.
Before the Session (Prep Phase)
Before the Session (Prep Phase): At the start of a session, the player uses the control panel to get their character ready. They review the Character Stats panel to ensure all information is up to date – for example, making sure HP is fully healed (if they rested at the end of last session) and that any resolved debilities are unchecked. The player can adjust these directly (recovering HP, clearing a “Weak” debility, etc.) through the interface. Next, they manage their equipment for the coming adventure: via the Inventory and Equipment panels, the player may swap gear or update items. For instance, if the character is in town at session start, the player might unequip a heavy armor and equip a lighter outfit; the interface immediately reflects those changes in the Armor stat and weight load. They can also add any new items acquired between sessions (like potions bought in town or rewards from last session) using the Add Item function so that the inventory is current.
Additionally, the player sets up the session tools they anticipate needing. They might open the Session Tools drawer and create trackers for expected events (e.g. a “Torch Timer: 6 turns” countdown or a “Stoneskin spell – 2 armor left” buff tracker) and jot quick notes about their goals or reminders for the session. For example, the player could set a tracker “Ritual incantation – 5” to count down a timed event, or note a buff like “Stoneskin (2 armor remaining)” so they remember its effect. If the character is a spellcaster beginning a new day in-game, the player also uses the Moves panel to mark prepared spells (toggling on which spells are prepared, with the interface showing how many slots are used). The player may glance at the Journal panel to refresh themselves on the story – reading the last session’s entry to recall where things left off. By the end of the prep phase, the character sheet is updated, gear is set, and any necessary notes or trackers are in place, so the player is fully ready to dive into the action.
During the Session (Action Phase)
As the game action unfolds, the player will frequently switch between viewing moves, rolling dice, and updating the character state. The Moves panel is central during this phase – for example, when the character attempts an action like Hack and Slash, the player simply clicks that move and the system rolls 2d6 plus STR, immediately showing the result and outcome. If the outcome is a failure, the interface highlights it (perhaps with red text or an “✗”) and automatically increments the character’s XP by +1 to reward the miss. The player can then narrate the result and proceed according to the move’s outcome. If the character takes damage, the player uses the interface to apply it: they might use the dice roller to roll the enemy’s damage (e.g. 1d8), then press the HP “–” button to subtract that amount from their HP. The HP display updates at once and may flash or change color if HP is low. Should HP drop to 0, the system immediately alerts the player that the Last Breath move is triggered (for example, displaying a pop-up or highlighting the HP in a critical color). Similarly, when the player uses an item mid-game – say their character drinks a healing potion – they can click a “Use” button next to that item in the Equipment panel. The app will deduct one use from the potion, possibly prompt to recover the specified HP amount, and update the item’s status (if uses drop to 0, the potion might disappear or be marked as expended). All these interactions are designed to resolve within one or two clicks, keeping the gameplay pace brisk and minimizing distraction from the story.
Throughout play, the interface allows the user to access information quickly without disrupting the flow. The persistent left sidebar means the player can always see crucial stats (like current HP, XP, and any active debilities) even while looking at other panels. For example, after a battle the player might want to recall a clue or NPC name – they can open the Notes or Journal panel to find that information while still keeping an eye on their HP and status, thanks to the always-visible HUD. The session log continuously records key events (roll results, damage taken, XP gains, etc.), so if the player needs to remember what happened a few minutes ago, they can scroll the log for a quick recap. The custom trackers in the Session Tools also come into play dynamically: if the character gained some “hold” from a move like Defend, the player might have a tracker “Defend hold: 1” on the side. Later, when they spend that hold to reduce an incoming attack’s damage, they decrement that tracker – when it reaches 0, they know that resource is used up (the interface might highlight or remove the tracker automatically at zero to show it’s expended). The UI is optimized for rapid context switching; many common actions have keyboard shortcuts (for example, pressing “M” to open the Moves panel, “I” for Inventory, “T” for Tools) to speed up navigation for advanced users. Overall, during the action phase the player is essentially cycling between the Moves, Stats, Inventory/Equipment, and Tools panels, and the design makes this effortless – they rarely need to dig through menus or pause the game to find something, which keeps the focus on the fiction and action.
End of Session (Wrap-up Phase)
When the session concludes, the control panel guides the player through end-of-session bookkeeping in alignment with Dungeon World’s rules. The player activates the End of Session sequence (for example, by clicking an “End Session” button or selecting the End of Session move in the Moves panel). The interface then walks them through the required steps: it first displays all of the character’s Bonds and asks the player to check off any bonds that were resolved during the session, automatically awarding +1 XP for each resolved bond. It then shows the character’s Alignment (or Drive) and asks if they fulfilled it this session; if yes, another +1 XP is granted. Next, it presents the three standard end-of-session questions (“Did you learn something new and important about the world?”, “Did you overcome a notable enemy?”, “Did you loot a memorable treasure?”), allowing the player to answer Yes or No to each. For each “Yes” answer, the system adds the appropriate +1 XP. Once these questions are answered, the interface sums up the total XP earned in this wrap-up and adds it to the character’s XP tally, showing a brief summary (e.g. “End of Session: You earned 3 XP”). At this point, the system immediately checks if the character’s new XP total meets the threshold to level up (which is when total XP ≥ current Level + 7). If the character can level up, the app notifies the player and can initiate a Level Up workflow. In the level-up process, the player is prompted to increase their character’s level by one, subtract the required XP (the app will handle resetting the XP to 0 or subtracting the exact amount, carrying over any extra), choose a new move from their class (or input a new move manually if needed), and increase one of their ability scores. The interface then updates all relevant stats (for example, if an ability score was increased, its modifier recalculates automatically). The player can also choose to defer leveling – if so, the app will simply remind them next time that a level-up is available. Either way, the end-of-session sequence ensures the character’s progression is properly accounted for before the next session.
After handling experience and potential leveling, the player can record the session’s events in the Journal. The interface encourages the user to write a new journal entry summarizing what happened in the adventure – for example, writing Session 5’s log with details of key events, discoveries, and unresolved plot threads. The log of dice rolls and any notes they took during play can be referenced to help compose this summary. Once the journal entry is saved, the player also performs any needed clean-up using the app: they might clear out any temporary trackers that are no longer relevant (for instance, removing a countdown tracker that reached 0), mark off any consumables used during the session in the Inventory (e.g. deduct rations or ammo that were spent), and add any new loot or items gained to their inventory. The system will have auto-saved all these changes, but the player can also choose to manually export their updated character data as a backup at this point. With that, the session is formally concluded – the character’s state is fully updated and preserved, and the player is ready to start the next session with all information carried forward. The guided end-of-session experience turns what could be an overlooked chore into a smooth, guided task, ensuring the player reaps all rewards (XP, loot) and updates their character properly before the next adventure.

Technical Architecture
•	Front-End Structure: The application is built as a single-page web app (SPA) for responsiveness and speed. It employs a modern JavaScript framework (e.g. React) to manage a dynamic, interactive UI. The interface uses a modular layout with a persistent sidebar and a dynamic main content area – the left sidebar stat panel is always rendered, while the central panel swaps between views like Moves, Inventory, Journal, etc., and a collapsible right sidebar holds session tools. This layout is designed to be responsive: on larger screens the user sees multiple columns at once, whereas on smaller screens side panels can collapse into icon-based toolbars or slide-out drawers to maintain usability. The consistent SPA architecture ensures the user experiences instant updates (no full page reloads) and a desktop-like fluidity during play.
•	UI Components: Each major part of the interface is encapsulated in a component, making the system modular. For example, there is a StatsPanel component for the left HUD, separate components for the Moves list, Inventory list, Equipment list, Journal viewer, etc., and a ToolDrawer component for the right-hand tools. Navigation between these is handled by a top menu or tab bar; selecting a section loads the corresponding component into the central area. We maintain a consistent visual hierarchy (e.g., section tabs or icons) so users can easily switch panels. Modal dialogs are used for focused interactions like editing an item, viewing a move’s full description, or performing the level-up workflow – these overlays appear on top of the main interface and can be dismissed to return to the game. This component-based structure ensures a clear separation of concerns (inventory logic lives in the Inventory component, etc.), making development and future extensions easier. It also means new panels or features can be added later without disrupting the overall layout or other components.
•	State Management & Data Model: The app uses a centralized state store to hold all character data, ensuring that all UI components always reference the same up-to-date information. We treat the character’s entire sheet as a single JSON-like object in memory, which includes fields for basic info (name, class, level, alignment), stats (attributes with their scores, modifiers, and debility flags), current HP and max HP, current XP, armor value, damage die, carrying capacity and current load, the list of moves and spells, inventory items, journal entries, trackers, and notes. (See the Appendix for a detailed schema.) Whenever any part of this state changes – for example, the user equips an item or gains XP – the change is immediately reflected across the UI because components subscribe to the state. Derived values are computed from this state as needed: for instance, encumbrance status is derived from currentLoad vs maxLoad, and whether the character can level up is derived from currentXP vs the threshold (level+7). We encode such game formulas in the logic so the app can automatically evaluate them. The global state is also serializable, meaning we can easily save it to local storage or export it (e.g., as JSON) and later reconstruct the character – essential for the save/load functionality. This single-source-of-truth approach guarantees consistency (e.g., if a move adds +1 Armor, both the stat panel and any other relevant part of the UI update together) and simplifies implementation of rule logic across the app.
•	Data Persistence: The application preserves the character’s data automatically so that progress is never lost. In the MVP, data will be stored locally in the user’s browser (for example, using localStorage or IndexedDB). This means every change (e.g. adjusting HP or adding an item) can trigger an auto-save to local storage, ensuring the character sheet is kept even if the user closes the app or refreshes the page. The user will also have the option to manually export their character data to a file (JSON format) and import it later – useful for backups or transferring the character to another device. In future iterations, we will introduce cloud synchronization and user accounts: a backend service with APIs will allow characters to be saved to a cloud database so a user can log in from multiple devices and retrieve their data securely. However, even with cloud sync, the system will remain offline-first – all core features (including dice rolls and rule calculations) run entirely on the client – so the app can function without internet and sync when convenient. This persistence architecture ensures that no matter what happens (browser crash, network drop, etc.), the character’s data is safe and can be restored.
•	Dice Rolling Engine: A built-in dice rolling module handles all random roll operations within the app. When a move is triggered or the user requests a custom roll, the engine uses a pseudo-random number generator to simulate the dice outcome. For move rolls, it automatically adds the relevant stat modifier and passes the total result back to the interface for display. All roll results (for moves or custom rolls) are recorded in a visible log, providing transparency and a history of outcomes. Because the dice roller is implemented client-side, there is no latency or dependency on external services – rolls work instantly and offline. The implementation will ensure true randomness and could include additional niceties like dice graphics or animation in future enhancements (though these are cosmetic and do not affect functionality). By having an integrated roller, the product removes the need for external dice apps, keeping the player’s focus in one place.
•	Rules Engine & Validation: The system includes a rules logic layer that encodes Dungeon World’s mechanics and validates state changes. Key game rules – such as how modifiers are calculated from stats, how load limits work, when XP triggers a level-up, and what happens at 0 HP – are implemented in this layer. For example, we define a function to compute an ability’s modifier from its score (using Dungeon World’s standard table) and apply a -1 if a debility is active on that stat. We implement checks like “if currentLoad > maxLoad, set encumbered status” and “if XP >= level+7, flag level-up available.” These rules are invoked automatically when relevant: e.g., after an inventory update the encumbrance check runs, or after an XP change the level-up check runs. By centralizing the game logic, we ensure consistency across the app; every feature uses the same source of truth for rules. This also makes maintenance easier – if a rule needs to be adjusted, it’s changed in one place. During development, we will test these rules thoroughly with unit tests and by running scenario simulations to make sure they align with the Dungeon World SRD. Notably, the app’s design still allows the player to override or manually adjust things when needed (for instance, they can edit a stat or XP if a house rule applies), but the default behavior is that the app will handle all the bookkeeping according to the official rules.
•	Performance & Scalability: The application is optimized to handle real-time updates efficiently. Using a reactive front-end framework means that only necessary components re-render when the state changes (for instance, updating an item will re-render the Inventory list and the Load display, but not re-render unrelated parts like the Moves list). The amount of data (one character’s info) is relatively small, so operations are fast and DOM updates are lightweight. Even as the character accumulates a long session log or many journal entries, we can manage performance by lazy-loading or paginating these if needed (e.g., only the latest part of the log is shown unless the user scrolls up for more). The app is expected to run smoothly on any modern desktop or tablet browser. In terms of scalability, because it’s mostly client-side, we can support many users simply by hosting the static app – each user’s device does the work. If we introduce a backend for cloud save, it will be a lightweight service primarily handling data storage, which can be scaled with standard cloud infrastructure as user count grows. The architecture also easily allows managing multiple characters per user in the future (e.g., an account with many saved characters) by extending the data model and UI to select different characters – this would not affect the core logic, just the way data is organized. Overall, the technical choices (SPA, client-heavy processing) keep the app scalable and responsive as features expand.
•	Infrastructure: Initially, the product can be deployed as a static web application that users load in a web browser. This means minimal server infrastructure – essentially just a web host or CDN serving the HTML/CSS/JS files. The app itself runs entirely on the client. This approach ensures high availability and low maintenance, since there’s no complex server stack to manage for core functionality. If and when we add cloud features in Phase 3 (user accounts, online storage), we will introduce a backend component. That backend could be implemented as a set of RESTful or GraphQL APIs with a database. It will handle tasks like user authentication and storing/retrieving character JSON data. We will design this backend for security (ensuring only authenticated users can access their data) and reliability, possibly using cloud services (for example, Firebase or AWS Lambda with DynamoDB) to simplify deployment. Importantly, the app’s offline-first design means that a temporary loss of backend connectivity won’t hinder the user – they can continue playing and the data will sync when the connection is restored. In summary, the infrastructure plan starts very simple (client-side only) and evolves to a modest client+server model as needed, always keeping the user experience uninterrupted and data safe.
Development Roadmap
1.	Phase 1 – MVP: The first phase delivers a Minimum Viable Product focusing on essential gameplay functionality. In this MVP, we will implement the core interface and features required to run a Dungeon World character: the persistent Character Stats panel (displaying stats, HP, XP, etc., with live updates and basic editing controls), the Moves panel with the ability to perform dice rolls for moves and display the outcomes, basic Inventory and Equipment management (adding items, equipping/unequipping items with immediate updates to stats like Armor and load), a simple dice roller for miscellaneous rolls, and a basic notes section for quick jotting. All underlying systems – the single-page layout framework, centralized state management, and local save/load capability – will be in place. This phase will yield a usable digital character sheet: a player can create or input their character, perform moves with automated dice results, track their hit points and experience, manage their gear, and have their data saved. Certain advanced features will remain manual in the MVP to reduce scope: for example, the end-of-session questionnaire and level-up procedure will be handled by the player manually (they can use the notes or adjust XP themselves), though the app will allow them to record those changes. The emphasis in Phase 1 is getting a functional product that proves the concept and can be play-tested in actual sessions, providing immediate value and feedback for further development.
2.	Phase 2 – Expanded Features: In the second phase, we will build on the MVP by adding the remaining core features and key automations to move from a basic tool to a full-fledged digital play assistant. This includes implementing the guided End-of-Session sequence (a user flow that asks the end-of-session questions about bonds, alignment, etc., and automatically awards XP based on answers) and the integrated Level-Up workflow (the app will detect when a level-up is possible and walk the player through increasing level, choosing a new move, and raising a stat). We will introduce the Journal panel, allowing players to create and save session logs within the app. Additionally, we’ll enhance existing features: for example, expanding the Moves panel to include a library of all class moves and spells (so players don’t have to type them in), and providing a user interface for spell preparation and casting for spellcaster characters. The trackers in the Session Tools will be refined to handle a broader range of use cases (e.g., allowing labels and max values, so they can serve as countdown clocks or resource meters more effectively). Visual and feedback improvements that were deferred in MVP will also be tackled – for instance, implementing color-coded alerts (HP bar turns red when low, an encumbrance icon appears when overloaded), confirmation dialogs for destructive actions (deleting items or journal entries), and more informative tooltips and help text where needed. By the end of Phase 2, the application will cover the full cycle of play for a single Dungeon World character with minimal manual effort: from character creation and mid-session moves to end-of-session leveling and journaling. We will also use this phase to respond to user feedback from the MVP, polishing workflows that might have been cumbersome and ensuring the app is user-friendly and robust.
3.	Phase 3 – Future Enhancements: After the core product is solid, Phase 3 will focus on enhancements that extend the platform’s capabilities and refine the user experience further. One key enhancement will be cloud integration: implementing user accounts and cloud saving so that players can log in and sync their characters across devices. This will involve building a backend and ensuring secure storage of character data online. Another area is collaboration and sharing – for example, allowing a player to easily share a read-only link or export of their character sheet with a GM or group, or possibly enabling real-time update sharing if multiple players use the app (though direct multi-user features are beyond the single-player scope for now, we may lay groundwork for it). We will also implement advanced UX improvements: full keyboard shortcut support for all common actions (to complement the ones added earlier), the ability to customize the interface (such as theme, font size, or panel layout adjustments to suit user preference), and adding polish like animation or sound effects for dice rolls and level-up events to enhance immersion. Performance optimizations may be addressed here as well – although the app will likely already be performant, we will profile it with large amounts of data (e.g., extremely long journals or logs) and optimize any slow points. Additionally, Phase 3 can explore cross-system flexibility – making the code more modular so that it could potentially support games similar to Dungeon World with minimal changes, which could broaden the user base (this might involve abstracting some rules engine components). All Phase 3 enhancements will be implemented in a way that builds upon the established architecture: for instance, cloud sync will use the existing serialization of state, and new cosmetic features will use the existing event hooks. By the end of Phase 3, the product will not only be feature-complete for its original vision but also enriched with convenience features, broader accessibility, and the infrastructure to support a growing user base.
Logical Dependency Chain
1.	Foundation Setup: Begin by setting up the project skeleton and core architecture. Choose the framework and tools (e.g. initialize a React project), and implement the basic application shell: a main page that loads the global state management and displays a simple layout (header, an empty sidebar, and an empty main panel). Establish the character state structure and ensure that it can be saved and loaded (initially, saving to local storage for testing). This step sets the stage for all future development by ensuring we have a working environment and a way to persist data.
2.	Stats Panel First: Develop the Character Stats & Health panel as the first functional component. This includes creating the UI for displaying character name, class, level, alignment, stat scores and modifiers, HP and XP, etc., along with interactive controls (e.g., buttons to adjust HP, checkboxes for debilities). Hook these controls up to the state – for instance, clicking “-HP” should decrement the hpCurrent in state and update the display. By doing this step early, we validate our state management and rendering loop. It also provides a visual foundation on the interface that will always be present, which helps when building and testing other features (we’ll always see if stats change correctly).
3.	Moves Panel & Dice Rolling: Next, implement the Moves panel and the core dice rolling functionality. Start with a subset of moves (for example, a few basic moves hardcoded or entered manually) to get the UI in place. Implement the roll mechanic such that clicking a move triggers a 2d6 roll plus the associated stat modifier, and display the result in a log area or in context on the panel. Also implement the logic to mark XP on a miss at this stage. This step turns the app into an interactive tool where a user can input an action and see an outcome. Achieving this early is crucial – it provides something testable and demonstrates the app’s value. It also lays the groundwork for handling any roll in the system (we can reuse the dice roller for other panels like a generic roll in the Tools).
4.	Inventory & Equipment Management: With moves and basic stats operational, proceed to implement inventory management. Develop the Inventory panel for listing carried items and the Equipment panel for equipped items. Set up the ability to add a new item (a simple form that updates state), edit items, and delete items. Implement the equip/unequip toggle: when an item’s “equipped” status changes, move it between the Inventory and Equipment lists and update the character’s stats (e.g., adjust armor or weight). This step depends on the Stats panel (to reflect weight and armor changes) and on the state infrastructure for items. Building it now allows integrated testing: for example, equipping armor should immediately bump the Armor stat in the Stats panel – we confirm that the panels communicate through state correctly. By completion of this step, the user can manage gear and we have covered a large portion of character data manipulation.
5.	Session Tools (Dice & Notes): Implement the Session Tools drawer with at least the dice roller and notes components. The dice roller UI can be a simple input (or set of buttons) for selecting dice types and a roll button; it will use the rolling function developed in the Moves panel to produce results, logging them to the same or a similar log. The Notes component can start as a single text area bound to the state (for a scratchpad note). This feature is relatively self-contained and can be done after core stats/moves/items are working, to enhance the app’s usefulness. It also ensures that the mechanism for opening/closing the right-hand drawer is in place and working, which is good to verify now. Once this is done, the user can do all primary in-game actions: roll any dice (via moves or custom), manage stats and equipment, and record notes – meaning we have a functional tool for playing through a session.
6.	Automations and End-of-Session: With the core interactive features in place, now focus on the advanced game logic automation. Implement the End-of-Session workflow: create a function or modal sequence that runs through bonds, alignment, and the 3 questions, updating XP accordingly. This will involve building a small UI (could be a modal or dedicated panel) that pulls data from the character (bonds list, alignment, etc.) and lets the user respond, then adjusts state (XP and maybe marks bonds resolved). Similarly, implement detection of level-up: each time XP changes, compare it to level+7 and if threshold crossed, flag in state that a level-up is available. Implement a Level-Up modal that triggers when the user chooses to level up – allowing them to increment level, choose a new move (this might be a dropdown of moves or a text input if we don’t have a full move database yet), and increase one stat. This step ties together many pieces (XP, moves, stats, etc.), so it naturally comes after those are ready. We will also, in this step, refine other automations: ensure the XP-on-miss is fully functional (if not already), ensure HP 0 triggers are handled (maybe by showing the Last Breath move or an alert), and that encumbrance warnings are working. Essentially, this is the phase where the app becomes intelligent: it starts proactively reminding the player of game rules. This is one of the most technically complex steps (logic-heavy), so doing it after establishing the simpler functionalities ensures we have a solid base to plug into.
7.	Journal & Additional Features: After the heavy lifting of automation, implement the Journal panel. This involves creating a form for adding a new journal entry (with a title or date and a content field) and a list to display past entries. Its interaction with other parts is minimal (mainly just state storage), so it’s safe to add towards the end. We’ll also take this time for any other feature that was not core to gameplay but desirable – for example, a character creation or import mechanism (even if just a form to input initial stats), or a Settings menu for options like enabling/disabling certain automations or switching theme. By now, all primary gameplay functions are done, so this step is about completing the package and providing the supporting features that round out the user experience.
8.	Polish and Refinement: Finally, conduct a thorough polish pass. This includes fixing any bugs discovered during integration testing of all features together, refining the UI styling (spacing, typography, color highlights for warnings, etc.), and improving performance if any lag is noticed. We add helpful tooltips or guidance text where users might need it (for example, hovering over a debility checkbox could show what it means). We also implement any remaining minor enhancements from user feedback – for instance, if playtests showed that a certain workflow was unintuitive, we adjust it now. This stage may also involve writing documentation or an in-app help section, so users know how to use each feature. Because we built the app in a modular, phased way, by this point we have been able to test and adjust each component, so polishing is about consistency and usability tweaks rather than major changes. After this step, the product should be ready for launch or a broader beta, with confidence that each feature is working correctly and the overall user experience is smooth and engaging.
Risks and Mitigations
•	Complexity of Full Rule Implementation: Risk: Incorporating all of Dungeon World’s rules and edge cases into the app could lead to bugs or misapplications if not done carefully. For example, miscalculating an XP threshold or overlooking an encumbrance rule could disrupt gameplay. Mitigation: We will closely adhere to the official rules and the design document when coding mechanics, ensuring formulas and conditions match the source material. Each piece of rule logic will be tested in isolation (unit tests for functions like modifier calculation, level-up check, encumbrance check) and in integration (full session simulations) to catch errors. We will also allow manual overrides for critical values – for instance, XP and HP can be edited by the user – as a safety net, so if an automation fails or a house rule is in effect, the player can still proceed. Clear logging of automated actions (e.g., “Marked +1 XP for miss”) will help users trust and verify the app’s calculations, and any discrepancies reported will be fixed promptly.
•	Scope Creep and Prioritization: Risk: The project includes many potential features, and there is a risk of trying to do too much at once or diverting into non-essential features (scope creep), which could jeopardize timely delivery of a working product. Mitigation: We have defined a strict phase-wise scope in this PRD. We will prioritize delivering a functional MVP (Phase 1) before adding enhancements. New feature ideas or improvements that come up during development will be logged and scheduled for future phases instead of being added immediately. Regular check-ins will be used to ensure development tasks align with the current phase’s goals. By focusing on the logical dependency chain, we ensure foundational features are completed and stable before layering additional functionality. This disciplined approach – build the basics first, then iterate – mitigates the risk of an ever-expanding scope and keeps the project on track.
•	User Interface Complexity: Risk: With multiple panels, menus, and data displays, there’s a risk the interface could overwhelm or confuse users if not designed clearly. A cluttered or unintuitive UI might discourage adoption. Mitigation: We are following a clear, familiar layout model (mirroring a paper character sheet’s sections) so that users instinctively know where to find things. During design and implementation, we emphasize simplicity: showing only relevant information in each panel and using visual cues (icons, section headers) to guide the user. We’ll conduct usability testing with a few target users to gather feedback on navigation and clarity. Adjustments like increasing font sizes, improving contrast, or rewording labels will be made based on testing feedback to improve readability. The interface will also be responsive, ensuring that even on smaller screens the user can access each panel easily (via collapsed drawers or swipe gestures) without losing context. By launch, the UI will be refined to be as straightforward as possible – accessible to new players while still offering depth for experienced users.
•	Data Loss or Stability Issues: Risk: A bug or crash could potentially cause character data to be lost or corrupted, which would be a serious issue for users investing time in their campaign. Also, unstable software (frequent errors or crashes) can erode user trust. Mitigation: We will implement robust data persistence with frequent auto-saves and backups. For example, the app can keep a backup of the last known good state and even use browser storage versions or cloud backups to recover if something goes wrong. We will thoroughly test the app under various scenarios (fast input, simultaneous updates, offline/online transitions) to catch stability issues. Using a proven framework and following best practices (avoiding memory leaks, using try/catch around file operations, etc.) will further reduce crash likelihood. Additionally, we provide users with the means to export their character data – encouraging periodic manual backups especially in early releases. In case a bug is reported, our data model’s simplicity will help in writing migration scripts or recovery tools (e.g., if an older version saved data slightly differently, we can detect and adapt). By prioritizing data integrity in development and providing user-facing backup options, we greatly mitigate the risk of serious data loss.
Appendix
•	Character State Schema: The character’s data is stored in a structured object. Key fields include:
•	Basic Info: name, class, level, and alignment of the character.
•	Attributes: Six ability scores (STR, DEX, CON, INT, WIS, CHA), each with its score and derived modifier, plus a flag for any debility on that stat (which imposes a –1 modifier).
•	HP: Current hit points (hpCurrent) and maximum hit points (hpMax).
•	XP: Current experience points (xp). This will be compared against the level-up threshold (which is calculated as level + 7) to determine when a level up is possible.
•	Armor: The character’s total Armor value (typically the sum of armor from equipped gear, possibly modified by moves or spells).
•	Damage Die: The base damage die for the character (e.g. "d8" for a fighter’s damage, stored as a string or code representing the die).
•	Load (Encumbrance): Carry capacity (maxLoad) and current carried weight (currentLoad). maxLoad is determined by the character’s class and strength (often class base + STR modifier in Dungeon World). The app flags encumbrance states if currentLoad exceeds maxLoad (encumbered) or exceeds maxLoad + 2 (overburdened).
•	Moves: A list of move objects representing the character’s moves. Each move might include a name, a description or effect text, and an associated stat (if it’s a rollable move) among other flags (e.g. whether it’s a starting move, an advanced move, etc.).
•	Spells: For spellcasting classes, a list of spell objects. These would include details like spell name, level, and whether it’s currently prepared (for classes like the Wizard/Cleric who prepare spells). In our model, spells can be treated similarly to moves or kept separate for clarity.
•	Inventory: A list of item objects representing everything the character carries. Each item has fields such as name, description, quantity, weight (per unit), tags (special properties or keywords), uses (number of uses or charges, if applicable), a boolean equipped flag, and a type (categorizing the item as weapon, armor, gear, etc.). Equipped items are also reflected in the Equipment panel, but under the hood they remain part of this list with equipped=true.
•	Journal: A list of journal entry objects for session logs. Each entry might have a title or timestamp, and a content body of text. This allows the player to keep a history of what happened each session.
•	Trackers: A list of tracker objects used for custom counters. Each tracker could include a title (what it’s tracking, e.g. “Torch light”) and a current value, plus an optional max value (for cases like countdowns or out-of N uses situations).
•	Notes: A freeform notes field (or a list of note entries) for any miscellaneous information the player wants to jot down outside the structured fields. This might be used for temporary session notes or reminders.
This schema is serialized to JSON for saving and can be extended in the future if needed. It is designed to cover all character-related information so that a single saved state contains the complete snapshot of a character at a given time.
•	Dungeon World Rule References: (for developer clarity and verification)
•	Level-Up XP Requirement: To level up, a Dungeon World character needs XP equal to their current level + 7. The app uses this rule to determine when to prompt the user with a level-up opportunity. For example, a level 2 character hits level 3 at 2 + 7 = 9 XP.
•	Encumbrance Thresholds: Dungeon World defines encumbrance based on Load. If a character carries more weight than their Load score, they are Encumbered (which can incur penalties), and if they carry more than Load + 2, they are Overburdened (significantly impeded). The app continuously calculates carried weight and will visually indicate these states (e.g., highlighting the load number in yellow for encumbered, red for overburdened).
•	Move Outcomes: Moves in Dungeon World use 2d6 plus a stat. Results of 10+ are a Success, 7–9 are a Partial Success, and 6- is a Miss (failure). The control panel follows this exactly: when a move is rolled, it will label the outcome (perhaps with symbols or color-coding) and, on a Miss, automatically prompt the player to mark XP (which the app also auto-adds to the XP tally). This ensures consistency with the “fail forward” XP mechanic of the game.
•	Last Breath Move: When HP is reduced to 0, the Last Breath move is immediately triggered (roll 2d6 with no modifier). Our app recognizes when HP drops to zero or below and will alert the user, possibly popping up the Last Breath move description and a roll button. This is to ensure the dramatic moment of life-or-death is not missed and is handled according to the rules.
•	Damage Rolls and Armor: Damage in Dungeon World is typically a separate roll (often by the GM for monster damage). The app’s dice roller allows the player to roll damage dice (d4, d6, d8, etc.) as needed. The Armor stat reduces damage taken – our app doesn’t automatically apply damage (since the user may want to narratively decide who takes it or how), but it provides the Armor value prominently so the player can subtract it. For instance, if a monster deals 5 damage and the character has 2 Armor, the player knows to only subtract 3 HP, which they can do with the HP “-” controls. The app may in future guide this (“Apply damage: 5 – Armor 2 = 3?”) but initially it leaves the decision to the user with the info provided.
All these rules are built into the application logic and UI prompts to closely adhere to Dungeon World mechanics. This ensures that using the app feels in harmony with the tabletop experience, with the added benefit that the app handles calculations and reminders, allowing players to focus on the storytelling.
